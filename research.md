	<h3 class="paper__title">Where Should Science Funding Go? Estimating Marginal Returns to Public Funding Across Research Fields</h3>
	<details class="paper__more">
		<summary>Abstract and paper details</summary>
		<p><strong>Authors:</strong> Francois Bares<br>
		<strong>Date:</strong> October 2026</p>
		<p><strong>PDF:</strong> <a href="{{ '/assets/papers/where-should-science-funding-go.pdf' | relative_url }}">Download paper</a></p>
		<p><strong>Presented at:</strong> Oxford FAME seminar; NBER productivity seminar; BDI&ndash;EIEF&ndash;CEPR Conference on Finance and Innovation.</p>
		<p><strong>JEL codes:</strong> O30; O32; O38; O51; O25; H4; E13; L52<br>
		<strong>Keywords:</strong> economics of science; public research; research policy; public research grants; sectoral planning</p>
		<p><strong>Abstract:</strong> Science funding is shrinking in many countries, while commercial innovation increasingly relies on publicly funded science. I study the optimal allocation of science funding across fields. I estimate marginal returns to funding in 19 biomedical fields using a micro-founded structural model where scientists compete for grants. These scientists differ in productivity and shift their research focus towards better-funded fields. Once funded, they produce knowledge by combining grant funding with existing knowledge from their own and other fields, giving rise to knowledge spillovers. Marginal returns vary widely across fields: an additional $1 million yields 2.2 publications in Nursing Research and 14.8 in Human Genome. Knowledge spillovers account for most of this gap. Upstream fields, such as General Medicine and Human Genome, generate far larger spillovers than specialised downstream fields, such as Nursing Research or Deafness and Communication Disorders. Scientists do move towards better-funded fields, but their replacements are equally productive once funded, so this reallocation costs little in equilibrium. I construct three welfare-relevant measures of scientific output: academic impact, commercial potential and disease-burden reduction. Marginal-return rankings barely change across the three measures. I combine each measure with the estimated marginal returns from the model to compare the current allocation with two counterfactual allocations: allocating funding in proportion to disease burden, as the medical community often advocates, and in proportion to marginal returns, as the model suggests. Across the three measures, allocation based on the model estimates yields 39&ndash;54% more output than the current allocation and 15.1&ndash;26.4% more than disease-burden alignment.</p>
	</details>

	<h3 class="paper__title">Does Artificial Intelligence Democratize Science? Evidence from AlphaFold</h3>
	<details class="paper__more">
		<summary>Abstract and paper details</summary>
		<p><strong>Authors:</strong> Francois Bares, Anantha Divakaruni, and Ludovic Phalippou<br>
		<strong>Date:</strong> October 2026<br>
		<strong>Earlier title:</strong> AI-qualizing Science</p>
		<p><strong>PDF:</strong> <a href="{{ '/assets/papers/does-ai-democratize-science.pdf' | relative_url }}">Download paper</a></p>
		<p><strong>JEL classifications:</strong> O33, O31, I23, L17, O34<br>
		<strong>Keywords:</strong> artificial intelligence; science; inequality; structural biology; AlphaFold</p>
		<p><strong>Abstract:</strong> Scientific output in resource-intensive fields is concentrated in a small number of elite universities. We study whether an open-access artificial intelligence tool reduces this concentration, using the public releases of AlphaFold, the deep-learning system that predicts protein structures. In the two years after AlphaFold was released openly, the share of the average low-ranked university in top-journal protein research rose by 50 percent relative to that of the average top-ranked university. The gains are absent outside protein research and in lower-tier journals. Papers from lower-ranked universities also become more novel and depart further from their authors’ prior work. Lower-ranked universities with in-house structural biologists rely less on collaboration with top-ranked universities. The evidence suggests that open-access artificial intelligence lowers the human and financial resources required to produce frontier science.</p>

	<h3 class="paper__title">Innovation Prizes as Fundraising Signals: Evidence from XPRIZE Competitions</h3>
	<details class="paper__more">
		<summary>Abstract and paper details</summary>
		<p><strong>Authors:</strong> Francois Bares, Thomas Hellmann, Elaine Hungenberg, and Andrew Tauhert<br>
		<strong>Date:</strong> October 2026<br>
		<strong>Status:</strong> Preliminary draft, please do not cite or circulate without the authorisation of the authors.</p>
		<p><strong>PDF:</strong> <a href="{{ '/assets/papers/innovation-prizes-as-fundraising-signals.pdf' | relative_url }}">Download paper</a></p>
		<p><strong>JEL classifications:</strong> L26, G11, O31, M13<br>
		<strong>Keywords:</strong> Venture capital, Entrepreneurship, Investment decisions, Innovation prizes, New firms and startups</p>
		<p><strong>Abstract:</strong> While a prior literature established that patents have signalling value to early-stage investors, little is known about the signalling value of innovation prizes. This paper analyses PitchBook fundraising outcomes of 383 startups that participated in innovation prizes offered by the XPRIZE Foundation, the largest organiser of innovation prizes in the world. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences estimator with a large control group, the analysis finds that XPRIZE participation increases the probability of raising equity in any quarter by 7.7 percentage points, relative to a baseline of 4.7%. Consistent with signalling theory, the effect is twice as large for companies without patents. It is positive and significant in each individual prize, but larger for prizes with larger prize purses. There is no evidence that prizes crowd out adjacent technology areas; instead, they provide a modest crowd-in effect. Startups participating in an XPRIZE competition attract new investors who had not previously invested in the specific technology area addressed by the prize. Equity investors respond more than Public grant funders. Overall, the results suggest that participation in an innovation prize provides a positive signal to investors, especially in nascent technological areas where alternative signals aren’t readily available.</p>
	</details>
